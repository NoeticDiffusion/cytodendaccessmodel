Yes. For **\(M_b\) specifically**, I think the dataset question should be sharpened considerably. We are not looking for “memory-related neural activity” in general; we need evidence for a **persistent branch-specific state that changes later recruitability after the immediate activity has disappeared**.

That is the empirical property \(M_b\) is supposed to capture. In the current model, \(M_b\) is explicitly the **slow structural-accessibility** variable, distinct from fast branch activity \(x_b\), fast spine/access state \(s_i\), and eligibility \(E_b\). :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"} Importantly, our existing DANDI work does **not** identify \(M_b\) itself: 000718, 000336 and 001710 are downstream observable bridges, and the manuscript explicitly preserves that boundary. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

A newly available dataset has made the situation substantially better.

### 1. My first choice now: Maristany de las Casas et al., G-Node 2026

**“Tuft dendrites in frontal motor cortex enable flexible learning”** is unusually well matched to the *functional* prediction of \(M_b\). The public G-Node archive contains behavioral, electrophysiological, two-photon calcium and glutamate imaging, opto/chemogenetic data, and Neuropixels from apical tuft dendrites in ALM during rule learning. The archive is only about **461 MiB** and is CC BY 4.0. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

The particularly valuable part is Figure 4. It contains **the same L5b dendrites tracked longitudinally across five sessions**, with:

\[
A \rightarrow A \rightarrow A \rightarrow B \rightarrow A
\]

in the relearning condition, versus

\[
A \rightarrow A \rightarrow A \rightarrow A \rightarrow A
\]

as a stability control. The data include branch-level calcium activity, selectivity and coding status over the five sessions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

That gives us almost a tailor-made test for a slow accessibility variable.

The question I would freeze would be:

> **Does branch-specific history predict later recruitment when the same rule returns, beyond current input, current activity and ordinary representational stability?**

Operationally, define something like

\[
H_b=
f(Y_{b,1}^{A},Y_{b,2}^{A},Y_{b,3}^{A})
\]

as the previous-history state of branch \(b\). Then predict its Day-5 response when Rule A returns:

\[
Y_{b,5}^{A}
=
\beta_0
+\beta_H H_b
+\beta_4Y_{b,4}^{B}
+\beta_{\mathrm{fast}}X_{b,5}
+\beta_{\mathrm{global}}G_5
+u_{\mathrm{animal}}
+\epsilon .
\]

The important quantity is **not** whether Day-1 and Day-5 activity correlate. That is too easy. The test is whether branch identity/history gives **held-out predictive information** about later recruitability after accounting for current-rule response, recent Day-4 activity and global session gain.

I would compare four arms:

| Model | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Fast-only** | Current cue/activity predicts branch response |
| **Global-history** | Animal/session history matters, but branches are interchangeable |
| **Branch-history** | Specific previous history of branch \(b\) predicts later reuse |
| **Branch-shuffled** | Same history statistics, assigned to the wrong branches |

The critical signature would be

\[
\text{Branch-history}
>
\text{Fast-only}
\]

and

\[
\text{Branch-history}
>
\text{Branch-shuffled},
\]

with a stronger or qualitatively different effect around the **A→B→A switch** than in the A→A→A control trajectory.

That would be genuinely interesting because \(M_b\) predicts **path dependence**:

\[
P(Y_{b,t+1}\mid U_t,H_b)
\neq
P(Y_{b,t+1}\mid U_t)
\]

even when \(U_t\), the current rule, is the same.

The published study also independently establishes that dendrite-inhibiting NDNF interneuron activation impairs relearning and suppresses dendritic-shaft calcium while sparing local spine transients, and that excitatory tuft inputs show rule-dependent clustering. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"} That makes the whole dataset unusually rich for distinguishing **fast dendritic gating** from **longer-lived branch history**, although we would have to audit whether the manipulation and longitudinal-imaging cohorts overlap enough to make a joint causal analysis legitimate.

**My rating: 9/10 for a first empirical \(M_b\)-analogue experiment.**

### 2. Best dataset for the *structural* component: Dryad 63xsj3v68

Our previous audit identified this as the most anatomically direct resource: longitudinal in-vivo two-photon imaging of **CA1 dendrites and individual spines**, including repeated imaging of the same structures, plus awake/anesthetized conditions and neural-activity manipulations. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"} The public deposit describes longitudinal hippocampal dendritic-spine remodeling explicitly. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

Here we could ask whether individual dendritic segments possess a persistent latent propensity for subsequent spine retention/remodeling:

\[
P(S_{i,t+48h}=1)
=
\sigma(
\beta X_i
+u_b
+u_{\text{mouse}}
),
\]

where \(u_b\) is the branch-specific component.

The stronger test is whether \(u_b\) remains reproducible and predictive after controlling baseline spine morphology, local activity and animal-wide state, and whether destroying branch identity removes that predictive advantage.

That starts to look very much like an **empirical structural analogue** of \(M_b\).

Its major weakness is equally important: **there is no clean memory-learning/linking paradigm**. So it can support:

> persistent local structural state exists and predicts future structural availability,

but not:

> memory experience writes \(M_b\).

**Rating: 8/10 for existence of a slow branch state; ~5/10 for the memory-writing hypothesis.**

### 3. DANDI 000718 should remain the replay/linking arm

000718 is still extremely useful because it supplies the part that the structural datasets lack: longitudinal CA1 calcium imaging, NeutralExposure followed later by aversive learning, offline periods and cross-session registration. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

Our existing analysis already found modest enrichment of previously defined NeutralExposure core units during offline high-synchrony events, but much of the event response was shared by the population-burst baseline. So the retained result is deliberately only an **offline selective-reuse bridge**, not direct slow writing. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"}

I would not rerun the same endpoint looking for a larger effect. Instead, if we return to 000718, I would formulate the \(M_b\)-aligned question as:

\[
\text{offline history of unit/ensemble}
\rightarrow
\text{later probability of selective reuse/linking}.
\]

That becomes the **memory consequence** of the branch hypothesis rather than a putative measurement of the branch variable itself.

### 4. DANDI 001710 is already useful evidence, but shouldn't be asked to do more

Our current 001710 result is actually quite aligned with a slow-timescale prediction: SparseKO animals showed lower cross-day stability than Cre across 4–6 usable days, with the gap widening at longer lag; the primary SparseKO–Cre subject-level permutation test was \(p=0.009\). But it is channel-sensitive and the Ctrl comparison is weaker. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"}

That makes it an **internal validated observable consequence**, not a latent-variable identification result. I would keep it in the ledger rather than try to reinterpret it as \(M_b\).

## What I would do next

I would actually prioritize the **G-Node ALM dataset over the Dryad dataset now**.

The reason is subtle. Dryad is anatomically closer to “structure,” but the G-Node dataset gives us something that is harder to obtain: **identified dendritic branches + longitudinal recurrence + an explicit context/rule change + a same-rule control condition**.

That permits a far cleaner falsifier of the fundamental \(M_b\) proposition:

\[
\boxed{
\text{future branch recruitability}
\text{ contains branch-specific memory of past use}
}
\]

rather than simply:

\[
\text{spines change over time}.
\]

I would call the first experiment something like **SB-MB-001 — Longitudinal branch-history recoverability** and make it deliberately narrow:

**Primary hypothesis:** In held-out Rule-A observations, previous branch-specific history improves prediction of dendritic recruitment beyond fast/current state.

**Primary negative controls:** branch-identity shuffle, day-order shuffle, global-history-only model, AAAAA drift control.

**Strong result:** history is predictive specifically at the same identified branch and survives these controls.

**Stronger follow-up:** the effect depends on learning/relearning history.

**Still stronger:** a manipulation changes the persistent branch-history effect more than immediate branch responsivity.

Only that last stage starts moving us from “an \(M_b\)-like latent state is useful” toward “there is evidence for an \(M_b\)-like **write mechanism**.”

So I would separate the claims as:

**Established external result:** individual dendritic branches are longitudinally trackable and exhibit learning-/rule-dependent dynamics; apical tuft dendrites causally contribute to flexible relearning. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="10"}

**Internal validated result:** our simulator contains \(M_b\) and produces diagnostic slow-writing signatures; current DANDI bridges constrain downstream consequences but do not identify \(M_b\). :chatgpt-content-reference{index="11"}

**Next testable hypothesis:** a branch-specific history variable predicts future recruitment better than fast-only/global alternatives.

**Speculative interpretation if it passes:** that persistent latent branch state could be a biological counterpart of \(M_b\).

I think this new G-Node dataset is strong enough that **I would open a Stage-0 metadata/source audit on it before searching for more datasets**. It may be the cleanest empirical contact with the slow-branch idea we've found so far.
